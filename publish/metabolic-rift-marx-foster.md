The bourgeois revolutions are, by definition, the transition from agrarian to urban society. This transformation of social relations of production is also a transformation of the human relation with the physical space and is constitutive matter: steel and concrete substitute wood and soil as the physical elements that raise the _locus vivendi_. In this passage, the old balance between humans and nature is irrevocably destabilized.

The reading by John Bellamy Foster of Marx's analysis of the transformation from agrarian feudal to urban bourgeois society leads him to conceptualize a socioecological dynamic named _metabolic rift_. The movement of a great part of the population to the interior of walled _burgs_ require that the fruits of the earth be taken thither necessary for feeding all that people–a necessary condition for industrial work. That means, Marx and Foster reveal, that nutrients–potassium, phosphorus, nitrogen–are subtracted from the earth and transported to the city, leaving the soil poor and infertile, rendering the soil into sand, and fertile areas into deserts. The reason being that the food, processed by the intestines of the townsfolk, transformed into excrement, are never returned to the earth, but rather thrown to the streets and sewers. 

The metabolic rift that exposes the contradictions in the interactions of humans with nature contributes, leads, in the interior of the civil society, to the intensification of the antagonism between the city and the country, with political repercussions. Workers of the urban and agrarian industries, exploited by the same dynamic of wage work, constitute even today separate classes–proletariat and peasantry–with distinct interests. The impoverishment of the earth to feed the city dwellers caused by the dynamic of metabolic rift puts forth a hard challenge to the unification of rural and urban working classes in the direction of a social transformation.

Food, in this sense, as a fuel that adds vitality to men and women of the city, carries in itself its negative, the subtraction of nutrients from bosom of the earth. The rift occurs because, differently to the previous historical period, when the overwhelming majority of the population lived in the rural lands, now the positive and negative moments of the natural cycle occur in different locations, introducing an imbalance. The anabolism of human beings in the city, because the matter does not return to its origin, is unable to compensate the catabolism of the earth. 

> "Capitalist production, by collecting the population in great centres, and causing an ever-increasing preponderance of town population, on the one hand concentrates the historical motive power of society; on the other hand, it disturbs the circulation of matter between man and the soil, i.e. prevents the return to the soil of its elements consumed by man in the form of food and clothing; it therefore violates the conditions necessary to the lasting fertility of the soil" (Marx, Capital, Vol. I, Ch. 15, §10).

The old patriarch of French letters clearly saw the antinomy, when he interrupted his great novel to advance a long and melancholy exposition about the _l'égout de Paris_ and the bounty daily wasted.

> Those heaps of refuse at the street corners, those cartloads of muck jolted through the streets at night, those hideous sanitation barrels, those fetid flows of underground filth hidden from view by the paving stones—do you know what they are? They are flowering meadows, green grass, wild thyme and sage; they are game and livestock; the contented bellowing of great oxen at evening; fragrant hay and golden wheat; bread on your table and warm blood in your veins; they are health, joy, and life itself. Such is the will of that mysterious creation which is transformation on earth and transfiguration in heaven. (Victor Hugo, Les Misérables, Vol. V, Book 2, Ch. 1).  

Victor Hugo described the sending of organic refuse to the sea as a senseless waste of resources, _sans aucun but_, calculated to the approximate sum of twenty million per year. What does not enter the poet-master's accounts, however, is that capitalism is not a system whose end is the optimal allocation of resources. Rather, its end is the accumulation of wealth, in the form of industrial and commercial profits. The systemic failure caused by the metabolic rift creates a providential opportunity to produce new commodities to supply the industrial agrarian production, with a high demand for nutrients to fertilize its depleted soil. Enters capital, as a social force for the mediation of contradictions, in the form of chemical manufacturing industry, with a supply of new commodities: artificial fertilizers, sewage treatment products; in their production, new forms of appropriation of surplus value.

Before the introduction of modern fertilizers, produced from the mineral extraction of enormous volumes of solid rock in the periphery of the global market, the damages to the soil caused by the metabolic rift were compensated by the use of guano, a solified accumulation of bird excrement. British ships would cross the Atlantic to bring loads of this natural fertilizer harvested by exploited slave labour in the Americas, to the enrichment of the merchant class. This historical picture adds to the collection of representations of the "_cannibal capitalism" described by Nancy Fraser, that expropriates and exploits natural resources and human labour force to feed its autophagous appetite.
