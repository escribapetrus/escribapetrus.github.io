Informational computing has always had a fetishismic aspect that scientists and theoreticians did not ignore. It is no surprise that in the lectures for the MIT course "Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs" professor Sussman jokingly wore a genie's hat, and admonished his students to learn arcane signs to command the demon in the machine. In every joke there is an admission of guilt. 

> What makes AI expansion in these extreme terms seem unstoppable is a technological determinism rooted in a fetishism of AI, in which it is seen as embodying a pure computational logic, combined with the naturalization of market relations, which suggests that the new technology will inevitably be subordinated to the interests of capital accumulation. Indeed, it is the advent of AI as a new regime of computational power controlled by monopoly-finance capital that is the emerging matrix of class (and imperial) struggle in our time. (cf. Foster)

We want to illuminate the idea of fetishism as it appears in constructs such as "fetishism of ____".

# The magician's black box

The concept of fetishism is derived from ethnological investigations about the role of _magic_ in so-called primitive societies: men and women endowed with privileged forms of science and technique were capable of affecting the spirits and the subtle matter, and to transform reality. Observers that contemplate the magic, without the knowledge of its inner workings, are left dumbfounded, astonished by its power, and terrified of its wielder, and deem it phantasmic, out of this world. 

Magic does not disappear in the modern world, erased by scientific rationalism. Rather, it goes through a dialectical _éclosion_, where some of its ritualistic, animistic aspects are shed, while other substantive aspects are preserved: the appeal to truth, the dogmatism of established knowledge, the hypostasis of empirical investigation into a general method.

Fetishism as a magical experience is a familiar bridge to our experiences with new technologies. Modern systems of information are so complex that even for trained professionals, the reason why some action works the way it does often remains hidden under words that deflect from its content: a function f(x) -> y hides in this arrow a whole universe of physical and spiritual operations under an _abstraction_; the differences between radically different systems are leveled by a _transparent execution_; the division of responsibilities for different applications in an architecture imposes the consideration of whole systems as _black boxes_. These and other expressions reveal that the activity itself of programming – which is, in the language of Peter Naur, _theory building_, the synthetic construction of a body of knowledge using algorithms and data structures for representing real entities, _"the reflex of the real world" (cf. Marx), has an inescapable recognition of its negative, the infinite _known unknowns and unknown unknowns_. 

The programmer chooses to separate parts of the implementation, of algorithms and data structure, to not contemplate, by reasons of efficiency. Cryptography is the perfect example: while it is possible to invent from scratch and to write by hand hypercomplex algorithms for hashing and encryption, it is common knowledge that that is a bad idea, and it's better to use any of the industry standard algorithms freely available for every programming language out there. Modern artificial intelligence adds a new dimension to this problem space, as the unknowns are hidden behind blackout blinds, through which it is impossible to gaze: the autonomy of a Large Language Model to construct connections in its own terms means its operations cannot be known, even if we so desire. The only things we have access to are its outputs. In the Faustian contract of traditional programming, in which data structures and algorithms are defined by the programmer, he or she still retains a position of control over the machine. The advent of artificial intelligence makes Marx's analysis of the alienation of the human worker that turns the human in the factory environment 'an appendage of the machine' (cf. Marx, Comm. Man.), much more than a philosophical exercise of materialist inversion of Hegel's dialectics of the slave and the master, a naturalist painting of concrete reality.

# Metaphysical subtleties and theological niceties

Fetishism works as an analytical key for a plethora of social dynamics in which _appearance_ (Erscheinung) conceals the _essence_ (Wesen) of things, by means of a sort of mystic transubstantiation of the object, that acquires qualities that have nothing to do with its own concept. It makes things sound less like man-made objects and more like medieval holy relics. We should take care, however, to not pay excessive attention to this magical, fantastic aspect, lest we evoke the demon by calling its name, and end up as victims to a sort of _fetishism of fetishism_. It is much less important to apprehend in the objects of our experience the chimera of a supernatural character, than using this idea as an tool with which to expose real fractures in social dynamics, immanent contradictions in the rational development of capitalist system.

In the critique of political economy, for instance, it is not simply the magical power to enchant the masses of consumers, through tricks of illusionism like branding, advertisement and addictive substances, that Marx's analysis of the _fetishism of commodities_ intends to reveal. Rather, as a starting point of his investigation of the systemic functioning of capitalist production and circulation of commodities, he points out that these are perceived as having an innate attribute, value, which we comprehend as a contitutive quality of the thing itself. No counterspell can undo this binding. Like the magician's cloak that hides the levers that control the trapdoor whence the rabbit appears and disappears, the fetishism of value hides the social relations and productive forces necessary to the production of commodities. This sleight of hand where social relations are swapped with natural qualities enable the writing of post-hoc market laws where the exchange value is reified as a thing in itself.

> A commodity is therefore a mysterious thing, simply because in it the social character of men's labour appears to them as an objective character stamped upon the product of that labour; because the relation of the producers to the sum total of their own labour is presented to them as a social relation, existing not between themselves, but between the products of their labour... There it is a definite social relation between men, that assumes, in their eyes, the fantastic form of a relation between things (cf. Marx, I. I. I. §§4).

To give an alternative example other than the classic example of the fetishism of value in the commodity form of capital: the advent of new forces of imperial power and violent disputes for territorial dominion resurrected from the time of the Cold War a political discourse marked by a _nuclear fetishism_, according to which an artifact whose only concrete content is the power to produce a chain reaction of megatons of atomic discharge, is believed to be possessed of spiritual properties, like _deterrence of aggression_, and _guarantee of national sovereignty_, that have nothing to do with its immanent content. This is a fetishism of power (cf. Harrington).

An important aspect of fetishismic thought is the logical fallacy of causal reversion, where points of arrival of the analysis are taken as points of departure, and vice versa: _"so soon as it steps forth as a commodity, it is changed into something transcendent. It not only stands with its feet on the ground, but, in relation to all other commodities, it stands on its head, and evolves out of its wooden brain grotesque ideas" (cf. Marx). This is patent in Marx's analysis of the fetishism of value in commodities, when he does the critique of liberal political economy. Instead of basing the analysis of the wealth of society starting from the manner in which the production of artifacts is organized, viz. the social relations of production and the productive forces through which rough matter is extracted from the earth and transformed into useful goods, the analysis of Smith and his followers takes the commodity as given, ready for sale, and from its behaviour in the free trade, it is possible to derive the "market laws". These determine, in the balance of supply and demand, the prices of commodities, the wages and profits, which in turn determine the volume of production for the next cycle. The price, a contingent apparition of the value of the commodity in the market, is taken as having analytical precedence over the necessary components of production and circulation, namely, labor and capital.

The same reversal appears in the newer forms of fetishism, such as the technological fetishism of innovation. _Le bon sens est la chose du monde la mieux partagée_, and it tends to articulate the recent transformations in social dynamics as if they were caused by the advent of new technologies and devices. For example, the _platformization_ of all aspects of life, e.g. grocery shopping, art and entertainment, love and sex, are understood as new behaviours enabled by the handheld connectivity to the global network. In the sphere of work, for example, we perceive the phenomenon of the disappearance of the work shift, and the growth of a dynamic of people _always on the clock_, available for work from home, or even from commute, again thanks to handheld devices. # The death of politics

What characterizes, then, the fetishism of computer technology in its latest incarnation, artificial intelligence? It is also deemed possessed of spiritual attributes that exceed its material content. Computer programs appear to us as having a wonderful capacity to, by themselves, solve problems, tear down old structures and transform society for the better. Systems design substitutes politics as the sphere of mediation of conflicting interests in society: social demands can be requested and fulfilled by means of providing an increasing number of programs and devices that solve individual everyday problems, and a network where people are allowed to connect, request, consume. "Solutionism" is the term that Morozov uses to describe this fetishism of innovation: "problems are to be dealt with via apps, sensors, and feedback loops – all provided by startups" (cf. Morozov).

> This much, however, is clear, that the middle ages could not live on Catholicism, nor the ancient world on politics. On the contrary, it is the mode in which they gained a livelihood that explains why here politics, and there Catholicism, played the chief part. For the rest, it requires but a slight acquaintance with the history of the Roman republic, for example, to be aware that its secret history is the history of its landed property. On the other hand, Don Quixote long ago paid the penalty for wrongly imagining that knight errantry was compatible with all economic forms of society (cf. Marx, I. I. I. §§4, note 33).

In just the same way as in these previous historical examples, it should be clear to us how we cannot live solely on innovation.

Another fetishismic aspect of new technologies of information, that intensifies with artificial intelligence, is the question of security. The huge volume of data and the speed of transmission enable the creation of advanced surveillance systems: cameras in all environments, registers of locomotion of every step taken during the day, of all people. Such systems are understood as endowed with a special quality, the efficiency in solving and preventing the occurrence of crimes, through profiling, data crossing, and statistical prediction. _"As Oracle CEO Larry Ellison has said, these technologies allow for tracking and targeting everyone all the time. 'Citizens will be on their best behavior, because we’re constantly recording and reporting everything that’s going on. And it’s unimpeachable…because AI is monitoring the video'"_. What this reading fails to account for are socioeconomic causes of crimes. Fetishistic solutionism is a treatment that acts on the symptoms, but never at the causes of the disease.

> In shifting the focus of regulation from reining in institutional and corporate malfeasance to perpetual electronic guidance of individuals, algorithmic regulation offers us a good-old technocratic utopia of politics without politics. Disagreement and conflict, under this model, are seen as unfortunate byproducts of the analog era – to be solved through data collection – and not as inevitable results of economic or ideological conflicts (cf. Morozov).

Data, as a representation, is devoid of any political and class content. By erasing the class differences in society, under the rubber of a fetishism innovation, we lose sight of the real question of politics and society: _"It’s about who controls the economy and whether regular people have a say in the decisions that affect them"_ (cf. Taylor, Levin).

> The equalisation of the most different kinds of labour can be the result only of an abstraction from their inequalities, or of reducing them to their common denominator, viz. expenditure of human labour power or human labour in the abstract. The two-fold social character of the labour of the individual appears to him, when reflected in his brain, only under those forms which are impressed upon that labour in every-day practice by the exchange of products. In this way, the character that his own labour possesses of being socially useful takes the form of the condition, that the product must be not only useful, but useful for others, and the social character that his particular labour has of being the equal of all other particular kinds of labour, takes the form that all the physically different articles that are the products of labour, have one common quality, viz., that of having value (cf. Marx, I. I. I. §§4).

# Cognition

Even the critical discourse regarding technology is plagued by fetishistic language. For example, people talk and write about a _cognitive decline caused by AI_, for which, thought there are signs, there is no scientific consenus. If we take Marx's example from the section of Fetishism, he describes a fantastics scenario, _"could commodities themselves speak_, they would say..."_ (cf. Marx I I I §§4). There is didactic value in this absurd hypothetical. It serves as a vaccine, an instinctive trigger response against fetishistic language that gives vitality and social agency to inanimate, dead artifacts.

But beyond the problem of _brain rot_, there is a concrete and true aspect of the social phenomenon of cognitive decline. 

> The world in which we live is – as I have argued in ‘Words and Lesions’ – determined by ongoing violent lesions of billions, top-down events initiated by the ruling classes in their own interests. They function in vicious feedback with the new hegemony’s cognitive degradation which is easily channelled into nationalism, racism, and finally fascism (cf. Suvin).

# So what? 

We have established that the development of capitalist society is marked by the appearance of different forms of _fetishism_, or illusory attributes, articles of our faith. Now, to quote Miles Davis, "so what?".

> This critique of the should be (_Sollen_) enables us to situate three hegelian themes: 1. it highlights the _truth_ of the Present; instead of taking it as the '_sealed realm of darkness', it convenes to comprehend it as the conclusion of a process whose current intelligibility attests its 'conformity' to the Concept; 2. philosophical knowledge has nothing to prescribe, couldn't pretend to teach how the world should be, as if the world has waited for it to know how it should be in order to be true (and truth is the accordance of a content to itself, not to our representation): in other terms, dialectics is not normative, the same way that the thing does not wait for its advent to provide for itself a rational basis and to reduce the distance that separates it from its essence, it is the object that is rational in itself and it doesn't fall to knowledge to bring from outside the object a reason (cf. Arantes, L'Ordre du Temps, I, 6).
