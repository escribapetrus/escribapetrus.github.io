# Gotta catch'em up

Industrialization appears in the history of peoples in the XIX and XXth centuries as the _entering in a process_, like the turnkey that starts the engine and gets the car going. It is a marathon, a race where everyone has the chance to win, as long as they have done the necessary physical preparation, for which there is no better routine than the anglo-saxon recipe. Subjects (meaning entrepreneurs, companies, countries) are free to adopt the model they want; that is, any one but socialism, which violates the divine mandate of private property, and protectionism, which offends the good relations between civilized peoples. But before the race starts, they are advised: if you don't follow the training program, you will not be able to finish the race. "De te fabula narratur". As Marx admonished the Germans, so does Marcuse the peoples at the periphery of capitalism: "the transformation of underdeveloped into industrial societies must as quickly as possible discard the pre-technological".

It is in terms of _reaching a stage_ that the industrialization of so-called backwards countries is defined. Marcuse denies the possibility of any development of industrial society outside the norms of capitalism and then-present day communism[^marcuse-colonial]. As the paradigmatic example of Japan in the development of its industrial capitalism in the XIX century shows, the increase of productive output in industry was accompanied by a broader change of social behaviours, political institutions, aesthetics and habits of thought. Appreciation for German metaphysics grew, as noticeable in the "Kyoto School" of philosophers, while the role of "national studies" was removed from institutional and doctrinal function, and relegated to a position of aesthetical validation of a Japaneseness (Yamato-damashii) that served as ideological ornament for the imperial expansion that was not Japanese in essence, just capitalist. Capitalism is a form of thought and organization of production and society that runs over alternative forms (if we don't want to use the word primitive) of life if and only if they contradict its concept, its _impulse for existence_. This complete normative system operates, in _one dimensional_ society and man, at the levels both of subjectivity and objectivity, that is, the cognitive and moral as well as the institutional and economic[^marcuse-self-propelling-produtivity].

[^marcuse-colonial]: "Is there any evidence that the former colonial or semi-colonial areas might adopt a way of industrialization essentially different from capitalism and present-day communism?"

[^marcuse-self-propelling-produtivity]: "A population untrained in the values of self-propelling productivity, efficiency, and technological rationality...(and) not yet transformed into a labor force separated from the means of production."

In the periphery of capitalism, in what is now called the Global South, the marvelous accomplishments of advanced industrial society worked as a true sirens' call that charmed economists and philosophers. The frankfurtian critique of abstract vices of "one dimensional society", "instrumental reason", "commodification of art" could not cause fear greater than the allure of the concrete fruits of modern industry, like cars and refrigerators. It became, then, or was confirmed as such, central the problem of _backwardness_. By the 1930s Brazil arrived at the situation with which the Bolsheviks had to contend on the eve of the 1917 Revolution, the imperative of industrial development. When asked what "communism" meant, so did Lenin respond: "Soviet power, plus the electrification of the entire country". But while the soviets had debated whether, in order to improve the productive forces in the former Russian Empire, it was necessary to carry out a bourgeois revolution, or whether the socialists were capable of delivering this historical task, here in Brazil the debate was settled: it was necessary to grow Brazilian capitalism. So the ideology of _desenvolvimentismo_ was born. Socialism, or at least socialist revolution, was not necessary; the promises of capitalism were good enough. These promises were what a man like Getúlio Vargas set out to advance.

But it was not the attachment to superstitions or to a magical and hierarchical morality, to the relics of feudalism, that, as it was the case in western Europe, thwarted the development of capitalism _ultra aequinoxialem_. Quite the opposite, capitalism, marked by the expulsion of rural labourers from the common and small properties, and their transformation into wage labor force, and the conversion of the major part of the production into production oriented by the exchange value to the detriment of the use value of commodities, is the birthmark of colonial enterprises even before the advent of industrial capitalism. (Rephrase: explain the difference between European history transition from feudal (bond of the peasant to the land, mutual obbligations, personal hereditary hierarchies, favour exchangs, transitions into market economy of the bourgeoisie: movable goods and labour, alienable everything. Brazil, what it keeps (favour exhchanges), what it has that is modern (export profit-driven economy)).

By putting the problem of the people as the problem of backwardness, industrial capitalism comes as a perfect solution. The idol of acceleration is the steam engine, and it demands feeding. The instrumental rationality promises to realize a glorious set of promises: it may not be utopia, but, in the absence of that, productive increase and societal control are the next best things. We just need to adjust the clock. To keep the promise of abundance in the modern horizon, the first step was to replace the archaic feudal structures with modern, rationalized structures. In the periphery of global capitalism, this meant cleaning up the remnants of the colonial period, understood as major contributing factors of _backwardness_. In the 1930s, the problem of backwardness in the economic sense was put in terms of overcoming of the agro-export model and becoming an industrialised country, worthy of taking a seat at the concert of nations together with the others that completed the Second Industrial Revolution. In this transition from a primitive, "backwards" agrarian society towards the advanced society, what actually happens is not a transition where one model gives way to the other; rather than coexisting, the latter produces the former, and feeds on it.

In Brazil,tThe expression _desenvolvimentismo_, that until very recently was an old-fashioned relic buried in dusty volumes of political economy from the 60s and 70s, resurfaced with surprising vigour. From a _gauchiste_ position of marxist and keynesian theoreticians, with barely any force in the concrete political sphere, it is now being raised as a question, and maybe even advanced as a project by voices ranging from the left to the right. The professor of economics Bresser Pereira, wrote one of these volumes, a critical summary of the rise and fall of this ideology[^bresser-desenvolvimento-crise], but his economic policy as Minister of Finance after the nightfall of the military government was hardly a New Deal. Likewise, André Lara Resende, another intellectual/statesman and one of the key figures for economic policy at the dawn of neoliberalism in the tropics, now actively raises the flag of keynesian economics against the _ideological straitjacket_ of the economic orthodoxy, viz. austerity measures, high interest rates as a means for lowering inflation, stagnation of state investments, and spends ink and saliva in advocating the need for reinvestment in national industry and a stronger command of economy by the state.

[^bresser-desenvolvimento-crise]: L. C. Bresser Pereira, Desenvolvimento e Crise no Brasil, describes the industrial revolution in Brazil in the 1930s, the model of "substitution of imports", and the limits of the model in terms of Keynesian decreasing marginal efficiency of capital and the challenge of attaining the volume of investment required for carrying out the Third Industrial Revolution.

It is a sign of the times that this state capitalist, interventionist ideology is now being claimed, even if hypocritically, by groups that previously espoused the ideology of _anarchocapitalism_, a mix of vulgarized Austrian School political economy and online cult that gained some notoriety in the figure of Javier Milei, the superhero costume-wearing president of Argentina. The _Movimento Brasil Livre_, now running for political offices, abandoned this neofeudal ideology of absolutely free _human action_ of the individual, in favour of a discourse about industrial development and references to a New State, in reference to the dictatorship in Brazil that initialized the industrial capitalist process, _desenvolvimentismo_.


## Backwardness in Brazil

Beginning in the 1930s, when the Brazilian Critical Tradition made Brazil into a concept in order to understand its historical development, the problem of backwardness was centrally in the order of the day, precisely because the horizon showed the possibility of, if not socialism, at least of reaching the stage of productive and social development similar to the best parts of European and North American capitalism. The task at hand for the early Brazilian Critical Theory was to point out the failures in the _national formation_ and the necessary forms for its surpassing, in order to put the country on the tracks, in synchrony with the rest of global capitalism.[^sergio-buarque-europa]

[^sergio-buarque-europa]: "What is certain is that all the fruit of our labor or of our rest seems to participate in a system of evolution belonging to a different climate and a different landscape" (Sérgio Buarque, Raízes do Brasil, 1. Fronteiras da Europa).

In order to ignite acceleration, the first questions to ask are what are the obstacles working against it, and what is the fuel that can be used for energy. To answer this, Caio Prado Jr. was one of the first to surpass the European theoretical framework of history and political economy which described, as a totality, the formation of capitalism as the takedown of feudal institutions and productive structures. The "Brazilian critical tradition"[^brazilian-critical-tradition] was a first attempt at a theoretical conceptualization of Brazil liberated from the metaphysical contents of European philosophy of history that determined the world view of thinkers up to this point. The systemic analysis of pre-industrial Brazil revealed a political and economic structure with little to no semblance to feudal Europe, and therefore it was both nonsensical to conceive a theory of Brazilian society rooted in feudal structures. This made it necessary to discover or invent a new concept of Brazil. 

[^brazilian-critical-tradition]: The term traces back to Antônio Cândido, who saw in the writings of Gilberto Freyre, Sérgio Buarque, and Caio Prado Jr. the birth of a theoretical conceptualization of Brazilian society that was truly original and dialectic, in the sense of being able to think Brazilian society through categories drawn from its own formation rather than imported from European history. Cândido named the trio Gilberto Freyre, Sérgio Buarque and Caio Prado the trilogy of the formation.

The Formation of Contemporary Brazil describes colonial agriculture as a roll of negatives. It enumerates the instruments and techniques that it lacks, because it would be harder to characterize it positively, that is, by what it does have. It does not fertilize the land, and therefore it cannot reutilize it; new farm fields have to be opened by burning the brushes and felling the trees; for the fire to feed the sugarcane mills, it does not reutilize the sugarcane bagasse (dregs), but wood from the forest, and the lack of firewood is one of the reasons for the ruin and abandonment of the mills. Not even the manure of animals is used to fertilize the earth, in order to allow its reuse. The use of rudimentary techniques and tools such as the hoe and shovel, and the absence of more efficient techniques such as the use of the motor energy of water in mills, the expansion of rural frontier using fire (_queimadas_) instead of more efficient methods for reutilizing the land, such as fertilization and crop rotation, are described not as remnants of pre-capitalist modes of living, thinking, and working. Instead, the rudimentary techniques and the low level of productivity are much better explained as immanent attributes of the organic functioning of the capitalist system as it constituted itself in Brazil, as an enterprise oriented for the production and commerce of commodities in and for the world market.[^caio-prado-dev-agriculture]

[^caio-prado-dev-agriculture]: "The development of agriculture in the period we have in view (XVII and XVIIIth centuries), though considerable, is much more quantitative than qualitative. In the field of technical advancement, the progress of Brazilian agriculture in that time is practically null. It (Brazilian economy) converges in a single objective, the maximum and instantaneous profit, all attention and effort" (Caio Prado, Formação do Brasil Contemporâneo).

It could not have been the lack of technical and scientific knowledge that imposed such precarious conditions in the material instruments and productive forces. Brazil was a part of the Portuguese Kingdom, the realm of skillful European sailors and explorers, who had cultural connections even in the far reaches of the Earth, in Tokugawa Japan, and it is not unreasonable to suppose they had access to knowledge of modern agriculture. Even if many ways of working were learned from the natives, we cannot accept that the precariousness of the rudimentary state of agricultural technology was merely a remnant of the influence of a pre-technological period of culture.[^tecnicas-medievais]

Caio Prado raises three possible causes for the regressive state of technological development in the colonial period, characterised by primitive tools and techniques: the slave labour, the lack of interest of the colonial administration in integrating Brazil to the global market, and the incapacity of the productive process to receive the necessary investments for its virtuous reproduction. In the barbaric use of slave labor, man is reduced to a beast, and the minimal amount of intelligence is applied to the work. Industrialization depends, we know from the historical example of the development of European capitalism, on technical and scientific education, provided either by the businesses or by the state. The creation of public education to provide that necessary component of the productive system dissolves the cost for the business owner in the collective treasury. Slave labor was used exactly because of its low cost, therefore investing in its technical and professional training would unmake any sense of this form of labour expropriation. The appetite for maximising short term profits, mystified by the Austrian thinkers Mises and Hayek as the driving force of innovation, deincentivises investment in the education of the labor force, and blocks the building of structures required for technological advancement. "The low technical level of our agrarian activities, and the consequences it would have, should not be attributed uniquely to the incapacity of the colonizers (or the workers). It could, it is certain, get up to date with its competitors in other colonies, and reach its much higher standard. But it would still be lacking. The evil was deeper. It was in the system itself, a system of extensive agriculture that dismantled with prodigal hands a wealth that it could not replenish... The problem becomes much harder. If it is not simple to educate the settlers and the general population to improve their processes and advance their technique, much more costly it would be to modify a system" (Caio Prado Jr. Formação, A Grande Lavoura).

## Critique de la raison dualiste

The corollary of the early Theory of National Formation was a sort of _dualist reason_ that separates the process of formation of capitalist systems as an opposition of coexisting ancient and modern social structures. In the interior of a national singularity, sectors of industry and groups of people live in the modern world: they produce using high-end technology and consume the products recently launched in the market; meanwhile, different, separate sectors and groups live in the old days, using obsolete technology and governing life according to primitive norms and customs. The second one is a world where scientific rationality has not yet taken place. Just as in the individual dimension, so it is in the universal, where advanced nations to which the future has already arrived coexist in the global theatre with those still awaiting.

What in the 1930s appeared as a beautiful promise, by the 1970s morphed into an ugly recognition of reality. Economic and political analysis revealed a decreasing rate of growth in the capitalist powers, and an increasingly antagonistic struggle between the interests of capital accumulation and the expansion of the welfare state. In this new historical scene, the utopian or theological opening of possibilities was closed, and what became clear was not what we were supposed to strive for as a possibility for realizing in society, but rather that these objectives were out of play. The solar horizon desired by the Brazilian _desenvolvimentistas_ now showed a red moon, harbinger of catastrophe. 

In the process of formation of Brazilian industrial capitalism, the Consolidation of Labour Laws that guarantees basic rights for workers, e.g. minimum wage, vacation, that historically happened as a civilizational step, also reveals itself negatively as a useful instrument of domination for the national bourgeoisie. This is in line with the marcusian argument that the state of comfort in modern industrial society anesthetizes and douses the revolutionary impetus for the radical transformation of social structures. It is a peace treaty that is advantageous for the owners of capital. "The labour laws are part of a set of measures destined to institute a new form of accumulation... the conversion of huge masses of population in 'reserve army' adequate to the reproduction of capital was pertinent and necessary in the perspective of the form of accumulation that was being created or reinforced: it provided the median horizon for the business calculations, liberated from the perfectly competitive market... on the other side, the labor legislation equated by reducing, instead of increasing, the price of labor force. This operation of equating from the base reconverted even specialized workers to the situation of unqualified workers, and prevented the precocious formation of a dual market of labor force... the regulation of labor laws operated the reconversion to a common denominator of all categories that, instead of injuring the accumulation, benefitted it" (Oliveira, C. R. Dualista).

Just like elsewhere, the political-economic development of industrial capitalism in Brazil is marked by a process of expansion of a white middle class. To this group belong so many of the celebrated heroes of Hollywood, Luke Skywalker, Marty McFly. In both physical appearance and in the landscapes that constitute their world, these representations hypostatize the shape of the particular white middle class man as the universal man in modern society. The improvement of his quality of life, however, was not borne from the natural functioning of industrial society: it was the price for the purchase of his support for the system; even where it shows itself most politically and economically violent. In Brazil, for example, this phenomenon appeared in the form of a supposed _intermediary redistribution_[^bresser-comprar-classemedia] of income from the rich to the middle class, in the form of subsidised housing credit and high salaries, qualifying the period as the "economic miracle" – an expression that reveals more the subjective feeling of this urban middle class and the accounting sleight of hand of state bureaucrats than the objective reality of the country[^chico-redistribuicao-intermediaria]. The myth of "economic miracle" happens in the same time of violent wage suppression and expansion of the rural frontier, where workers were subject to barely subsistence-level income and life conditions, hunger and violence. Even up North, in the United States, the comfort level of the white middle class cannot be thought without its inseparable double of racial segregation and poverty.

[^bresser-comprar-classemedia]: Bresser Pereira advances this thesis in Desenvolvimento e Crise no Brasil. In his view, the road for industrial development in Brazil reached by the 1960s a turning point, that required the choice of a national economic model: the keynesian protectionist model, _interventionist state capitalism_, with massive public investment in the national industry and the expansion of demand at the base of the pyramid, or the orthodox model, with slashes to government spending and the opening of markets to the international capital. The choice was the second.

[^chico-redistribuicao-intermediaria]: Francisco de Oliveira rejects the voluntarist explanation of increased wealth of the middle class during the period of the "economic miracle" (post 1964). "There was no intermediary redistribution: the possibility that this may be feasible would terminate all the problems of capitalism...it is truer and easier to suppose that the elevated level of income of the middle classes is due to the new occupations created by the industrial expansion and the position of these occupations in relation to the productive structure in the global social scale". He rejects the idea that the military government bought the middle class by intentionally redistributing income to this group, as a means of gaining ideological support for its political position. The acceptance of such understanding of political economy means accepting the growth of inequality in capitalist society is a moral choice, and therefore open to fixing through a more enlightened management. It "gives the state apparatus a rationality it does not have, to operate the 'intermediary redistribution'. It is easier and truer to suppose that the higher level of income of the middle classes is a result of new occupations created by the industrial expansion and the position that these new occupations have in relation to the productive structure, in terms of global social scale" (Oliveira. C. R. Dualista, V).

The _Critique of Dualist Reason_ smashes the illusion that backwardness and modernity are separate moments in the formation of national and international capitalism, exposing them as simultaneous aspects of capitalism that feed on one another and are equally necessary for the _functioning_ of the capitalist unit. Chico de Oliveira, the epigone of the early Theory of National Formation, explains the problem of backwardness, the misery, and the failures in developing the national capitalism not by reconstitution of the historical genesis of the ethical character of Lusitanian society and the methods for settling the territory (Sérgio Buarque), or by the mechanism of productive forces and relations of production in the colonial political-economy (Caio Prado), but in a concrete analysis of the present society and economy, in the choices and tactics taken by the Brazilian business class[^fernando-henrique-empresariado] in comparison with other countries. He lifts the blinds and reveals that the real process of capitalist formation constitutes an organic totality, a _unity of opposites_, where the so-called advanced depends on the backwards for its reproduction. 

[^fernando-henrique-empresariado]: The critique is rooted in the sociological analysis of industrial business in Brazil by Fernando Henrique Cardoso: _Empresário Industrial e Desenvolvimento Econômico no Brasil_, 1964.

Even the marxist tradition, whose method is rupture and contradiction, not continuity, sees the development of nations in terms of temporal advancement, or _progress_, _arriving somewhere_. The first nations to turn the key and start the industrial machine (paradigmatically, England) arrived first; the others must accelerate their step to enter and advance in this race, where participation is mandatory. Marcuse, when discussing the transition from pre-industrial into modern industrial society describes the need to eliminate the primitive morals and institutions alternative to those of the _one dimensional society_. But what the Critique of Dualist reason reveals, through the study of the perfect case subject for an analysis of the hypothesis of transition from primitive to advanced industrial society, is that the political economy of Brazil is not divided between completed capitalist forms on one side and, on the other, dregs of precapitalist forms that have failed to adapt to the new mode of production — that is, failed to replace norms of thought and labour such as superstition and magical thinking with the technical and scientific rationality of the management of production and society. Instead, the truth is that these dregs are products of the capitalist system: "Pre-industrial economies in Latin America were created by the expansion of the capitalist world as a reserve of primitive accumulation in the global system; in short, _underdevelopment_ is a capitalist formation, and not simply historical" (Oliveira, C. R. Dualista).

This dynamic of dependence, where one organ feeds on the other, appears more recently in Nancy Fraser's description of _cannibal capitalism_ as an organism of reproduction through a dual mechanism of exploitation and expropriation. The process of primitive accumulation of capital does not end in the first period of formation or transition to capitalist society, which would then expand by itself. On the contrary, it is part of capitalist expansion, even in its later industrial phase, to repeat and open new forms of primitive accumulation in a continuous process of _expropriation_ that runs in parallel with the classic labour exploitation, like two heads of a hydra, constantly feeding itself on others, in the cyclic process of growth of cannibal capitalism (cf. Fraser, "Cannibal Capitalism"). In the periphery of capitalism, such duality between the primitive forms of expropriation and the modern industrial labor exploitation appears in purest form: "The expansion of capitalism in Brazil occurs through the introduction of new relations in the archaic and reproduction of archaic relations in the new" (Oliveira, C. R. Dualista, II).
