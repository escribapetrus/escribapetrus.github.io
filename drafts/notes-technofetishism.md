Informational computing has always had a fetishistic aspect that scientists and theoreticians did not ignore. It is no surprise that in the lectures for the MIT course "Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs" professor Sussman jokingly wore a genie's hat, and admonished his students to "conjure the spirits of the computer with our spells" (Sussman and Abelson, SICP, ch. 1). In every joke there is an admission of truth. 

# Metaphysical subtleties and theological niceties

The concept of fetishism is derived from ethnological investigations about the role of _magic_ in so-called primitive societies: men and women endowed with privileged forms of science and technique were capable of affecting the spirits and the subtle matter, and to transform reality. Observers that contemplate the magic, without the knowledge of its inner workings, are left dumbfounded, astonished by its power, and terrified of its wielder, and deem it phantasmic, out of this world. 

Magic does not disappear in the modern world, erased by scientific rationalism. Rather, it goes through a dialectical _éclosion_, where some of its ritualistic, animistic aspects are shed, while other substantive aspects are preserved: the appeal to truth, the dogmatism of established knowledge, the hypostasis of empirical investigation into a general method.

Fetishism as a magical experience is a familiar bridge to our experiences of new technologies. Modern systems of information are so complex that, even for trained professionals, the reason why some action works the way it does often remains hidden under words that deflect from its content: the arrow of the function f(x) -> y hides a whole universe of physical and spiritual operations under an _abstraction_; the differences between radically different systems are leveled by a _transparent execution_; the division of responsibilities for different applications in an architecture imposes the consideration of whole systems as _black boxes_. Programming, from the start, is the synthetic construction of a body of knowledge using algorithms and data structures for representing real entities; _theory building_, in the language of Peter Naur. But this "reflex of the real world" (Marx, Capital, Vol. I, ch. 1, §4) has the inescapable recognition of its negative, the infinite _known unknowns and unknown unknowns_. 

The programmer chooses to separate parts of the implementation, of algorithms and data structure, to not contemplate, by reasons of efficiency. Cryptography is the perfect example: while it is possible to invent from scratch and to write by hand hypercomplex algorithms for hashing and encryption, it is common knowledge that that is a bad idea, and it's better to use any of the industry standard algorithms freely available for every programming language out there. Modern artificial intelligence adds a new dimension to this problem space, as the unknowns are hidden behind blackout blinds, through which it is impossible to gaze: the autonomy of a Large Language Model to construct connections in its own terms means its operations cannot be known, even if we so desire. The only things we have access to are its outputs. In the Faustian contract of traditional programming, in which data structures and algorithms are defined by the programmer, he or she still retains a position of control over the machine. The advent of artificial intelligence takes Marx's analysis of the worker in the factory, made "an appendage of the machine" (Marx, Communist Manifesto, ch. 1), and, from a philosophical reflection on a historical process, turns it into a naturalist painting of concrete reality.

# A switch so quick the eye can't see

Fetishism works as an analytical key for a plethora of social dynamics in which _appearance_ (Erscheinung) conceals the _essence_ (Wesen) of things, by means of a sort of mystic transubstantiation of the object, that acquires qualities that have nothing to do with its own concept. It makes things sound less like man-made objects and more like medieval holy relics. We should take care, however, to not pay excessive attention to this magical, fantastic aspect, lest we evoke the demon by calling its name, and end up as victims to a sort of _fetishism of fetishism_. It is much less important to apprehend in the objects of our experience the chimera of a supernatural character, than using this idea as a tool with which to expose real fractures in social dynamics, immanent contradictions in the rational development of capitalist system.

In the critique of political economy, for instance, it is not simply the magical power to enchant the masses of consumers, through tricks of illusionism like branding, advertisement and addictive substances, that Marx's analysis of the _fetishism of commodities_ intends to reveal. Rather, as a starting point of his investigation of the systemic functioning of capitalist production and circulation of commodities, he points out that these products of human labor are perceived as having an innate attribute, value, which we comprehend as a constitutive quality of the thing itself. A shard of wood can, through the worship of the masses, produce miracles, and no chemical examination will disprove this power. Likewise, under the fetishism of value, it is hard to dispel the mist that hides the social relations and productive forces necessary to the production of commodities. 

> A commodity is therefore a mysterious thing, simply because in it the social character of men's labour appears to them as an objective character stamped upon the product of that labour; because the relation of the producers to the sum total of their own labour is presented to them as a social relation, existing not between themselves, but between the products of their labour... There it is a definite social relation between men, that assumes, in their eyes, the fantastic form of a relation between things (Marx, Capital, Vol. I, ch. 1, §4).

Let us shed light on the question with a different example, one that makes this idea of transubstantiation very obvious. The advent of new forces of imperial power and violent disputes for territorial dominion resurrected from the time of the Cold War a political discourse marked by a belief in the spiritual powers of the nuclear bomb. This technological artifact's immense destructive power to produce megatons of atomic discharge has the effect of acting as a _deterrent of aggression_ on the global stage, as no country would (we see in the news that this logic is not without flaws) take action against another in possession of an atomic bomb, as that would risk total human annihilation, or _mutually assured destruction_. Based on this _use value_ as a "destroyer of worlds", the atomic bomb acquires in the sphere of circulation an exchange value as a _currency of power_ (cf. Harrington, Nuclear Weapons) to purchase political influence and national safety. The fetishism of power leads to an absurd impulse to accumulate this currency that takes on a life of its own, and defeats its own purported intention of guaranteeing peace.

It will be noticed that an important aspect of fetishistic thought is the logical fallacy of causal reversion, where the points of arrival of the analysis are taken as the points of departure, and vice versa: "so soon as it steps forth as a commodity, it is changed into something transcendent. It not only stands with its feet on the ground, but, in relation to all other commodities, it stands on its head, and evolves out of its wooden brain grotesque ideas" (Marx, Capital, Vol. I, ch. 1, §4). This is patent in Marx's critique of "vulgar" economy (Vulgärökonomie) as corollary of his analysis of the fetishism of value. The metamorphosis of social relations into objective qualities enable the writing of post-hoc laws based on exchange value reified as a thing in itself. Instead of basing the analysis of the wealth of society starting from the manner in which the production of artifacts is organized, viz. the social relations of production and the productive forces through which rough matter is extracted from the earth and transformed into useful goods, the analysis of the later marginalist economists, Jevons, Menger and Walras, takes the commodity as given, ready for sale, and from its behaviour in the free trade, it is possible to derive the "market laws". These determine, in the balance of supply and demand, the prices of commodities, the wages and profits, which in turn determine the volume of production in the next cycle. The price, a contingent apparition of the value of the commodity in the market, is taken as having analytical precedence over the necessary components of production and circulation, namely, labor and capital.

> Les économistes ont une singulière manière de procéder. Il n'y a pour eux que deux sortes d'institutions, celles de l'art et celles de la nature. Les institutions de la feodalité sont des institutions artificielles, celles de la bourgeoisie sont des insitutions naturelles. Ils ressemblent en ceci aux théologiens, qui eux aussi établissent deux sortes de réligions (Marx, Misère de la philosophie, 1847, p. 113, quoted in Capital, Vol. I, ch. 1, §4, note 33).

# Imagining knight errantry compatible with all economic forms of society  

What does it mean to describe computer technology in its latest incarnations, Big Tech (2010s) and Artificial Intelligence (2020s), as fetishistic? Here, as in the previous examples, we see something claimed to possess spiritual attributes that far exceed its material content. Computer programs appear to us as having a wonderful capacity to, by themselves, solve problems, rebuild the old structures and transform society for the better. "Solutionism" is the term that describes this fetishism of innovation: "problems are to be dealt with via apps, sensors, and feedback loops – all provided by startups" (Morozov). _Systems design_ substitutes politics as the sphere of mediation of conflicting interests in society: social demands can be satisfied by the development and distribution of an increasing number of programs and devices that solve individual everyday problems, and a network where people are allowed to connect, request, consume. 

This attribution of a power of agency to programs and artifacts deeply influences the way we understand the recent changes in social dynamics. For example, the _platformization_ of all aspects of life, e.g. grocery shopping, art and entertainment, love and sex, is perceived as a behavioral change effected by our handheld devices. In the sphere of work, in turn, the phenomenon of the disappearance of the work shift, and the growing trend of people _always on the clock_, available for work from home, or even from commute, appears also as enabled by the handheld connectivity to the global network. It seems as if the smartphones, inanimate bricks of plastic and metal, did this by themselves, and we humans were defenseless prey to their schemes.

This framing of problems, by affirming that devices can by themselves determine the way we live, does away with all references to political and economic disruptions in the concrete conditions of life. But we can put the thing back on its feet by negating this inverted logic. 

1. Platforms that provide online services, such as remote medical appointments through video calls, and courses through video lectures, don't simply win space in the market because the UI is intuitive and it's more practical to do things from the computer. The privatization of essential services, such as health and education, and cuts to government programs for energy and housing, through the dismantling of the welfare state starting in the 1970s, allowed financial monopolies and big tech corporations to take over these industries and profit with the online sale of the basic necessities of life in civilized society.  
2. Social media and dating apps provide a digital space for socialization where completely new laws and algorithms influence the motion of the _affectūs_: e.g. the desire for followers, the uninterrupted broadcast of routine, and the _social health checks_ of likes. But none of this can happen without the ruin of communal life that births the absolute digital society (cf. Tönnies, _Gemeinschaft und Gesellschaft_); a historical process advanced by the closing of public spaces for noncommercial activities. The practice of sports is increasingly expensive in the cities. Prices for cultural events such as concerts and sports matches are prohibitive for the poor. Even stepping outside is expensive, and there isn't anything to do except shopping. In such an environment, there are fewer opportunities to meet people, to talk about life and grow healthy relationships.
3. Tech startups and hardware manufacturers have grown massively in the last decade, not just due to the financial market speculation and the expected profitability of digital products. The destruction of worker unions that strengthened the labor class in their demands for increased wages allowed a gigantic accumulation of wealth for the capitalist class. This, in turn, is invested in scientific research and computer infrastructure to create tools for automation and replacement of the work force. _"The very potential for the expansion of disposable labor time (leisure) due to automation, Marx explained in his day, contradicts capital’s incessant need to expand surplus labor time. The system therefore seeks to promote, by way of automation—based on the leverage provided by an expanding industrial reserve army—the increased degradation and material dependence of labor_ (Foster, §4)". 

Then, there is the question of security. It is acknowledged how, according to the Marxist analysis of capitalism, impoverishment of a big portion of society is desirable to the capitalist class as a means of reducing wages and increasing profits (cf. Marx, Capital, Vol. I, ch. 25). This reveals an innate contradiction in all policies for _fighting crime_: any effective long-term policy to reduce crime should also _fight poverty_, something to which there is no economic incentive in this system, where these failures are not bugs, but features. 

This does not prevent tech industry, through its mania for innovation, from taking this as an opportunity to capitalize, by selling products for solving the problems of violence and crime. The huge volume of _data_–a representation devoid of any concrete political and economic content-and the speed of transmission enable the creation of advanced surveillance systems: cameras in all environments, registries of locomotion of all people, during all day. Such systems are perceived as endowed with a special quality, the efficacy in solving and preventing the occurrence of crimes, through profiling, data crossing, and statistical prediction. _"As Oracle CEO Larry Ellison has said, these technologies allow for tracking and targeting everyone all the time. 'Citizens will be on their best behavior, because we’re constantly recording and reporting everything that’s going on. And it’s unimpeachable…because AI is monitoring the video'"_ (Foster). This proposal fails to take into account the socioeconomic factors at the root of urban criminality, and how poverty and disenfranchisement are main drivers of increase of levels of violence. Fetishistic solutionism is a treatment that acts on the symptoms, but never at the causes of the disease. 

# Conclusion

In all these domains, ranging from political economy to warfare, science and technology, we see a common thread, where top-down determinations and external contents are presented as intrinsic characters of things. This _appearance_ brushes away the concrete historical and material process through which these things are brought forth, full of unresolved contradictions.

 By covering the political and economic contents of society under the cloak of fetishism, we lose sight of the real issue (Sache). The real transformation should be directed at _"who controls the economy and whether regular people have a say in the decisions that affect them"_ (Taylor and Levin). Be them commodities, nuclear bombs, or computers, we cannot put faith in the magical power of artifacts to shape our world in a way that will solve problems that are, in truth, in the heart of society; a society made by, of and for humans, and therefore capable of change.
