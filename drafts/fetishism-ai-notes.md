Here are the key passages from Capital Vol. I, Chapter 1, Section 4 ("The Fetishism of Commodities and the Secret Thereof"), organized by what each one contributes to your essay:
1. The opening — "The table turns into a thing with a wooden brain"
"A commodity appears, at first sight, a very trivial thing... its analysis shows that it is, in reality, a very queer thing, abounding in metaphysical subtleties and theological niceties... So soon as it steps forth as a commodity, it is changed into something transcendent. It not only stands with its feet on the ground, but, in relation to all other commodities, it stands on its head, and evolves out of its wooden brain grotesque ideas, far more wonderful than 'table-turning' ever was."
Why it helps your essay: This is Marx's most vivid image of fetishism — the familiar object (wooden table) acquiring a life of its own. Your current draft jumps straight into the analytical definition of fetishism without this concrete, almost comical starting point. Opening your "Value" section with the dancing table would ground the abstraction.


2. The core definition — "A social relation between men takes the form of a relation between things"
"A commodity is therefore a mysterious thing, simply because in it the social character of men's labour appears to them as an objective character stamped upon the product of that labour; because the relation of the producers to the sum total of their own labour is presented to them as a social relation, existing not between themselves, but between the products of their labour... There it is a definite social relation between men, that assumes, in their eyes, the fantastic form of a relation between things."
Why it helps your essay: This is the mechanism of fetishism, not just the appearance. Your draft says "the fetishism of value hides the social relations" — Marx is more precise: it doesn't just hide them, it transforms them into something else (relations between things). This inversion is worth making explicit since it's the same operation you identify in technological solutionism (social conflicts reframed as technical problems).

3. The religious analogy and the naming of "fetishism"
"In order, therefore, to find an analogy, we must have recourse to the mist-enveloped regions of the religious world. In that world the productions of the human brain appear as independent beings endowed with life, and entering into relation both with one another and the human race. So it is in the world of commodities with the products of men's hands. This I call the Fetishism which attaches itself to the products of labour, so soon as they are produced as commodities, and which is therefore inseparable from the production of commodities."
Why it helps your essay: Your essay draws the same analogy (magic, religious relics) but doesn't cite this as Marx's own move. Directly referencing that Marx himself reaches for the religious analogy would strengthen your "magic does not disappear" argument (line 21) — showing that the continuity between "primitive" fetishism and commodity fetishism is Marx's own insight, not an imposition.

4. Causal reversion — "The producers are ruled by their own products"
"These quantities vary continually, independently of the will, foresight and action of the producers. To them, their own social action takes the form of the action of objects, which rule the producers instead of being ruled by them. The determination of the magnitude of value by labour time is therefore a secret, hidden under the apparent fluctuations in the relative values of commodities."
Why it helps your essay: You introduce "causal reversion" on line 35 — "points of arrival taken as points of departure" — but as a logical fallacy rather than a lived experience. Marx says it's not just a mistake in reasoning; it's how producers actually experience the market. The market really does confront them as an external force. Your point about "flexible working hours" (line 38) would land harder if framed this way: it's not that people misunderstand the cause, it's that the cause really does appear to them as a property of devices.

5. The historical alternatives — Robinson Crusoe, medieval feudalism, the peasant family, the community of free individuals
"Since Robinson Crusoe's experiences are a favourite theme with political economists, let us take a look at him on his island... All the relations between Robinson and the objects that form this wealth of his own creation, are here so simple and clear... Let us now transport ourselves from Robinson's island bathed in light to the European middle ages shrouded in darkness. Here... the social relations between individuals in the performance of their labour, appear at all events as their own mutual personal relations, and are not disguised under the shape of social relations between the products of labour... Let us now picture to ourselves, by way of change, a community of free individuals, carrying on their work with the means of production in common... The social relations of the individual producers, with regard both to their labour and to its products, are in this case perfectly simple and intelligible."
Why it helps your essay: Marx uses four counter-examples (Robinson, medieval feudalism, peasant family, communist association) to show that fetishism is historically specific to commodity-producing society, not a universal feature of labour. Your essay could use this — it implies that technological fetishism is similarly specific to capitalist society. Adding one of these contrasts (especially the "community of free individuals") would strengthen your implicit argument that another relation to technology is possible.



6. The political economy critique — "It has never asked why"
"Political Economy has indeed analysed, however incompletely, value and its magnitude, and has discovered what lies beneath these forms. But it has never once asked the question why labour is represented by the value of its product and labour time by the magnitude of that value."
Why it helps your essay: Marx's key move against the economists is that they take the form (value, price) as natural and only ask about its magnitude, never about why it exists at all. This is exactly the structure of your critique of solutionism — technologists ask "how do we make surveillance more efficient?" but never "why is the social problem being framed as a technical one?" This parallel would tie your two threads (value fetishism and tech fetishism) together explicitly.
